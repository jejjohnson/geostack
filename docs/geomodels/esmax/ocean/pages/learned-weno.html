Learned WENO: an advection scheme that learns where to dissipate

The advection scheme is where a coarse ocean model loses information: it smooths fronts, dissipates eddies, and decides whether a run stays stable. This page keeps everything that makes WENO safe (flux form, fixed candidate stencils, convex weights, land masks) and learns one thing: how to weigh the candidates at each face. Same Gulf Stream QG example as the other pages.

TL;DR

Fixed stencils, learned weights

  • Every advection scheme is a centred part plus a dissipation. The centred part moves information; the dissipation removes it. Fifth-order upwinding is exactly sixth-order centred plus a hyperdiffusion \(\vert u \vert \Delta x^5/60\).
  • A single learned kernel cannot work. Godunov's theorem: a linear scheme that never creates oscillations is at most first order. The weights must depend on the local field.
  • WENO already does that, by hand. It blends three small stencils with weights set by how smooth each one sees the field. Learned WENO adds a learned correction to those weights, starts from zero (so it starts as classical WENO5), and is switched off where the field is smooth.
  • Boundaries use the same mechanism. A candidate that reads a land cell gets weight zero. Near a coast only the safe candidates remain, so the stencil narrows on its own.
  • It is a familiar machine-learning object: soft attention, or a mixture of experts, over a fixed bank of local polynomial experts, scored by their roughness. The parallels section works each comparison through with the same numbers.
  • Every weight is smooth in the state, so the adjoint is the true linearisation of the forecast: important for 4D-Var.

The problem

The QG run of the other pages has \(\Delta x = 8\) km and a deformation radius \(L_d \approx 30\) km, so an eddy is about four grid cells across. The Gulf Stream itself is a PV front a few cells wide. Two classical choices both fail here:

SchemeWhat it does to the frontConsequence
Centred, high ordersharp, but rings at the front (grid-scale noise)noise feeds back through the velocity; needs strong explicit viscosity or it blows up
First-order upwindstable, smeared over many cellsa weak, wide jet; wrong separation latitude; eddies die early
WENO5sharp where smooth, safe at the frontthe best classical answer, with hand-chosen weights and dissipation

The question this page asks: keep WENO5's structure, and learn how much to trust each candidate stencil from a high-resolution run of the same ocean.

Flux form: conservation for free

Integrate \(\partial_t q + \partial_x(uq) = 0\) over cell \(i\). The cell average changes only through its two faces:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}q_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{F_{i+\frac12} - F_{i-\frac12}}{\Delta x}, \] \[ F_{i+\frac12} = u_{i+\frac12} \hat q_{i+\frac12} . \]

Summing over all cells, every interior face appears once with each sign, so \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_i q_i \Delta x\) depends only on the domain walls, where \(u=0\). Total PV is conserved whatever rule produces \(\hat q\). That is why the learning goes into \(\hat q\), never into a gradient: an advective form \(u \partial_x q\) with a learned gradient has no such identity.

Every scheme is a centred part plus a dissipation

Take the classical fifth-order upwind reconstruction for \(u\ge0\), on the points \(q_{i-2},\ldots,q_{i+3}\), and the sixth-order centred one on the same points:

\[ \hat q^{\text{up5}} = \frac{1}{60}\left(2q_{i-2} - 13q_{i-1} + 47q_i + 27q_{i+1} - 3q_{i+2}\right), \] \[ \hat q^{\text{c6}} = \frac{1}{60}\left(q_{i-2} - 8q_{i-1} + 37q_i + 37q_{i+1} - 8q_{i+2} + q_{i+3}\right). \]

Their difference is a fifth difference, so the upwind flux is the centred flux plus a term proportional to \(\vert u \vert\):

\[ u \hat q^{\text{up5}} = u \hat q^{\text{c6}} - \frac{\vert u \vert}{60} \delta^5 q_{i+\frac12}, \] \[ \delta^5 q_{i+\frac12} = -q_{i-2} + 5q_{i-1} - 10q_i + 10q_{i+1} - 5q_{i+2} + q_{i+3} . \]

For a wave \(q = e^{\mathrm{i} kx}\), the centred part only moves it (zero damping); the second part damps it at rate \(\vert u \vert \Delta x^5 k^6/60\) for \(k\Delta x \ll 1\). In continuous form, fifth-order upwinding is the exact equation plus a sixth-order hyperdiffusion:

\[ \partial_t q + u \partial_x q \approx \frac{\vert u \vert \Delta x^5}{60} \partial_x^6 q . \]

So "upwinding" is a choice of dissipation, and the dissipation is what decides stability and what the model forgets. The cleanest way to control it is to write any face flux in this split, with a non-negative face diffusivity \(\kappa\) (dimensionless, in units of \(\Delta x\)):

\[ F_{i+\frac12} = u \hat q_{i+\frac12} - \vert u \vert \kappa_{i+\frac12} (q_{i+1} - q_i), \] \[ \kappa \ge 0 . \]

With periodic or walled boundaries, the second term changes the variance \(\frac{1}{2}\sum_i q_i^2\) by \(-\sum_i \vert u \vert \kappa (q_{i+1}-q_i)^2 /\Delta x \le 0\). It can only remove variance. The learned \(\kappa_{\gvect{\theta}}\) on this page enters exactly here.

Why a single learned kernel cannot win

A learned gradient or face kernel with fixed weights is a linear scheme: \(\hat q = \sum_j w_j q_{i+j}\). Godunov's theorem says a linear scheme that never creates new maxima or minima is at most first-order accurate. Training a fixed kernel therefore picks a point on one trade-off curve:

Fixed kernelSmooth eddiesFronts
wide, high order, little dissipationaccurateoscillates; grid noise can grow
narrow or strongly upwindsmearedstable

A fixed kernel can still tune the shape of the error across wavenumbers (how much each scale is delayed or damped), and that is a useful baseline. To be sharp in smooth water and safe at the front at the same time, the weights have to depend on the local field. That is nonlinearity, and WENO is the classical way to do it.

Classical WENO5, from local basis functions

Read WENO the way a neural operator is read: move the data into a basis, act on the coefficients, evaluate the result where it is needed. Here the basis is local: one small polynomial per candidate stencil.

Cell averages and a local coordinate

A finite-volume model stores cell averages, \(q_j = \frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{\text{cell } j} q \mathrm{d}x\). Measure position from the centre of cell \(i\) in cell widths, \(\xi = (x - x_i)/\Delta x\). Cell \(i+j\) is then the interval \([j-\frac{1}{2}, j+\frac{1}{2}]\), and the face \(i+\frac12\) sits at \(\xi = \frac{1}{2}\). For \(u\ge0\) the face sees the window

\[ \vect{q} = (q_{i-2}, q_{i-1}, q_i, q_{i+1}, q_{i+2}) . \]

Step 1 · A local basis

Each candidate \(r\) is one quadratic, written in the monomial basis \(\gvect{\varphi}(\xi) = (1, \xi, \xi^2)^\top\) with its own coefficients \(\vect{a}_r\):

\[ p_r(\xi) = \sum_{m=0}^{2} a_{r,m} \varphi_m(\xi) = \gvect{\varphi}(\xi)^\top\vect{a}_r . \]

Candidate \(r\) is fitted on three cells: \(\mathcal{S}_1 = \{i-2, i-1, i\}\), \(\mathcal{S}_2 = \{i-1, i, i+1\}\), \(\mathcal{S}_3 = \{i, i+1, i+2\}\).

Step 2 · Lift: cell averages to coefficients

Ask the quadratic to have the right average on each of its three cells. The average of \(\gvect{\varphi}\) over cell \(i+j\) is \((1, j, j^2 + \frac{1}{12})\), so this is a 3 × 3 linear system, solved once:

\[ \mat{A}_r \vect{a}_r = \vect{q}_{\mathcal{S}_r}, \] \[ (\mat{A}_r)_{j} = (1, j, j^2 + \frac{1}{12}), \] \[ \vect{a}_r = \mat{E}_r \vect{q} . \]

\(\mat{E}_r\) is \(\mat{A}_r^{-1}\) placed into the columns of its three cells, a fixed 3 × 5 matrix. For the centred candidate the coefficients are a mean, a slope and a curvature:

\[ \vect{a}_2 = \left( -\frac{1}{24}q_{i-1} + \frac{13}{12}q_i - \frac{1}{24}q_{i+1}, \frac{1}{2}(q_{i+1} - q_{i-1}), \frac{1}{2}(q_{i-1} - 2q_i + q_{i+1}) \right). \]

Step 3 · Evaluate at the face

The candidate's face value is its polynomial at \(\xi = \frac{1}{2}\):

\[ \hat q^{r} = p_r(\frac{1}{2}) = \gvect{\varphi}(\frac{1}{2})^\top\mat{E}_r \vect{q} = \vect{c}^{r\top}\vect{q}, \] \[ \gvect{\varphi}(\frac{1}{2}) = (1, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}). \]

The same thing in a cardinal basis: \(\ell^r_j(\xi) = [\gvect{\varphi}(\xi)^\top \mat{E}_r]_j\) is the quadratic whose average is 1 on cell \(j\) and 0 on the other two. Then \(p_r(\xi) = \sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_r} q_j \ell^{r}_j(\xi)\), and every stencil coefficient is a basis function evaluated at the face: \(c^{r}_j = \ell^{r}_j(\frac{1}{2})\).

Step 4 · Smoothness is energy in the non-constant modes

The smoothness indicator measures the slope and curvature of each candidate over the face's own cell \(i\), in units of the cell width:

\[ \beta_r = \sum_{m=1}^{2}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}(\partial_\xi^m p_r)^2 \mathrm{d}\xi = a_{r,1}^2 + \frac{13}{3} a_{r,2}^2 = \vect{a}_r^\top\mat{S} \vect{a}_r, \] \[ \mat{S} = \mathrm{diag}(0, 1, \frac{13}{3}). \]

(\(a_{r,1}\) and \(a_{r,2}\) are the slope and half-curvature at the centre of cell \(i\); for candidates 1 and 3 that is the end of their stencil.) The constant mode has weight 0, so adding a constant to \(q\) never changes \(\beta\). This is the idea behind modal smoothness indicators in spectral methods: roughness is how much energy sits in the higher modes. In the window, \(\beta_r = \vect{q}^\top\mat{M}_r\vect{q}\) with \(\mat{M}_r = \mat{E}_r^\top\mat{S} \mat{E}_r\); expanded, these are the textbook formulas:

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_1 &= \frac{13}{12}(q_{i-2} - 2q_{i-1} + q_i)^2 + \frac{1}{4}(q_{i-2} - 4q_{i-1} + 3q_i)^2,\\ \beta_2 &= \frac{13}{12}(q_{i-1} - 2q_i + q_{i+1})^2 + \frac{1}{4}(q_{i-1} - q_{i+1})^2,\\ \beta_3 &= \frac{13}{12}(q_i - 2q_{i+1} + q_{i+2})^2 + \frac{1}{4}(3q_i - 4q_{i+1} + q_{i+2})^2 . \end{aligned} \]

Step 5 · Mix

The nonlinear weights favour smooth candidates, with \(\tilde\beta_r = \beta_r/\sum_s\beta_s\):

\[ \alpha_r = \frac{d_r}{(\tilde\beta_r + \varepsilon)^2}, \] \[ \omega_r = \frac{\alpha_r}{\sum_s \alpha_s}, \] \[ \hat q = \sum_{r=1}^{3}\omega_r \hat q^{r} . \]

With the linear weights, the mixture is exactly fifth-order upwinding: \(\sum_r d_r \vect{c}^r = \frac{1}{60}(2, -13, 47, 27, -3)\). In smooth water all \(\beta_r\) are similar and \(\gvect{\omega} \to \vect{d}\). Across a front, the candidates that straddle it have large \(\beta\) and get almost no weight. finitevolX normalises by \(\sum\beta\) (held out of the gradient), so rescaling \(q\) rescales \(\hat q\) exactly and nothing overflows in float32.

In DeepONet terms, the trunk is the fixed polynomial basis and the branch is \(\omega_r(\vect{q}) a_{r,m}(\vect{q})\). Every learned part of learned WENO sits in the routing \(\omega\); the basis, the lift and the evaluation stay fixed, and that is what keeps consistency and order.

The hand-chosen parts of classical WENO are the exponent 2, the floor \(\varepsilon\), and the linear weights. They decide where WENO dissipates, and they were tuned on shock-tube problems, not on ocean eddies.

Learned WENO

Write the WENO weights as a softmax over logits, then add a learned correction to the logits:

\[ \omega_r = \mathrm{softmax}_r\left(\underbrace{\log d_r - 2\log(\tilde\beta_r + \varepsilon)}_{\text{classical WENO5}} + \tilde\tau g_\text{corr}(\vect{e}; \gvect{\theta})_r\right), \] \[ \tilde\tau = \vert \tilde\beta_1 - \tilde\beta_3 \vert . \]

Since \(\mathrm{softmax}(\log\alpha) = \alpha/\sum\alpha\), the first term alone is exactly classical WENO5. The feature vector \(\vect{e}\) at each face holds only scale-free numbers:

\[ \vect{e} = (\tilde\beta_1, \tilde\beta_2, \tilde\beta_3, C, n^-, n^+). \]

Three design choices carry the safety:

  • Zero initialisation. The last layer of \(g_\text{corr}\) starts at zero, so the untrained scheme is WENO5. Training only moves away from it as far as the data asks.
  • Gating by \(\tilde\tau\). In smooth water \(\tilde\tau\to0\), the correction vanishes, and the weights return to WENO5's, which keeps fifth order where the field is smooth. The learning acts only near fronts and under-resolved eddies.
  • Pointwise network. \(g_\text{corr}\) is a 1×1 convolution over the feature axis: the same small function at every face. The scheme is translation-equivariant and transfers to other domains and grid sizes.

A second head gives the extra dissipation \(\kappa_{\gvect{\theta}} = \mathrm{softplus}(g_\text{diss}(\vect{e}; \gvect{\theta})) \ge 0\), added through the variance-safe term of the flux. For \(u \lt 0\) the window is mirrored (\(q_{i+3},\ldots,q_{i-1}\)) and the same network is applied, so the scheme has no preferred direction.

Land: the same mechanism

Candidate \(r\) reads three cells. If any of them is dry, its logit is set to \(-\infty\) before the softmax, and the remaining weights renormalise:

\[ \omega_r = 0 \ \text{ if candidate } r \text{ reads a dry cell}, \] \[ \sum_r \omega_r = 1 \ \text{ over the wet ones}. \]

In open water all three survive. One cell from a coast, only the candidate pointing away from land survives, and the face value is third order. At the coast itself the normal velocity is zero, so the flux vanishes whatever the reconstruction. finitevolX already does a hard version of this: Advection2D with a Mask2D picks, per upwind cell, the widest stencil tier whose cells are all wet (sizes 2 to 10), using the counts of contiguous wet cells in mask.stencil_capability. Learned WENO uses the same counts twice: to mask candidates, and as features \(n^\pm\), so the network can learn boundary-current behaviour (for example, more dissipation along the western boundary, where the grid is most under-resolved).

If the window reaches past the narrowest candidate (a single wet cell between two coasts), the face falls back to first-order upwind, as the finitevolX tiers already do.

Parallels

Strip WENO to its skeleton and it is a pattern machine learning uses everywhere: a few fixed local linear estimators, mixed by input-dependent normalised weights.

\[ \hat q = \sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_r(\vect{q}) \langle\vect{c}^r,\vect{q}\rangle, \] \[ \omega_r = \mathrm{softmax}_r(s_r(\vect{q})), \] \[ s_r = \log d_r - p \log(\tilde\beta_r + \varepsilon). \]

scores_attn = dot_scores / sqrt_dim + position_bias  # (R, H, W)

# --- WENO, same slot ---
# −p log(β̃ᵣ + ε): content score, "how smooth does candidate r see the field"
content = -temperature_inv * log_beta       # (R, H, W)
# sᵣ = log dᵣ − p log(β̃ᵣ + ε): bias log d plays the position bias
scores = einx.add(                          # (R,), (R, H, W) → (R, H, W)
    "candidate, candidate height width -> candidate height width",
    log_linear_weights,
    content,
)
# ωᵣ = softmax_r(sᵣ)
weights = einx.softmax("[candidate] height width", scores) #| (R, H, W)
# values h_val,r = q̂ʳ = ⟨cʳ, q⟩, fixed (never learned)
values = einx.dot(                          # (R, P), (P, H, W) → (R, H, W)
    "candidate point, point height width -> candidate height width",
    candidate_coeffs,
    window,
)
# q̂ = ∑ᵣ ωᵣ q̂ʳ
face = einx.dot(                            # (R, H, W) ×2 → (H, W)
    "candidate height width, candidate height width -> height width",
    weights,
    values,
)

What transfers: a learned temperature \(p\), per face, interpolates continuously between ENO, WENO and linear upwinding. Extra heads are extra candidate families (for example, trigonometric stencils tuned for eddies). And the rule that makes learned WENO safe: learn the scores, never the values.

Mixture of experts and conditional convolution

CNNs, sparse models

A mixture of experts routes an input to experts and mixes their outputs; conditional convolution mixes the experts' kernels and applies the result once. When the experts are linear, the two are the same, and WENO is both:

\[ \text{MoE:} \ \sum_r \pi_r(\vect{h}) f_r(\vect{h}), \] \[ \text{CondConv:} \ \left( \sum_r \alpha_r(\vect{h}) \mat{W}_r \right) \star \vect{h}, \] \[ \text{WENO:} \ \hat q = \sum_r\omega_r \langle\vect{c}^r,\vect{q}\rangle = \langle\vect{c}(\vect{q}), \vect{q}\rangle, \] \[ \vect{c}(\vect{q}) = \sum_r\omega_r \vect{c}^r . \]

So WENO applies a different 5-tap kernel at every face. On the two windows (all ×1/60; each sums to 1):

Kernel\(q_{i-2}\)\(q_{i-1}\)\(q_i\)\(q_{i+1}\)\(q_{i+2}\)
linear upwind 52.00−13.0047.0027.00−3.00
WENO5, smooth window1.78−12.6647.3426.22−2.66
WENO5, front window2.86−18.5758.5717.140.00

At the front the kernel stops reading \(q_{i+2}\) and leans upwind. Treat each face's kernel as if it were used everywhere, and its Fourier symbol gives the damping rate of a wave \(e^{\mathrm{i}k x}\) (per unit \(u/\Delta x\); negative is damping):

Kernel\(k\Delta x = 0.5\)1.52.5
linear upwind 5−0.0002−0.107−0.779
WENO5, smooth window−0.0008−0.127−0.813
WENO5, front window−0.0025−0.276−1.576

The front kernel damps near-grid-scale waves about twice as hard: WENO's dissipation, made visible.

# Shapes: R = 3 candidates (candidate); P = 5 points (point);
#         K = 64 test wavenumbers (wavenumber); H, W grid

# --- mixture of experts: mix the outputs ---
# q̂ = ∑ᵣ ωᵣ ⟨cʳ, q⟩
face_moe = einx.dot(                        # → (H, W)
    "candidate height width, candidate height width -> height width",

# --- diagnostic: local Fourier symbol of c, a map of numerical damping ---

What transfers: the kernel view gives a diagnostic for free. Map the local damping rate of a trained learned WENO over a snapshot to see where it dissipates. Sparse top-k routing from MoE would be cheaper, but its switches are not smooth, so keep soft routing wherever an adjoint is needed.

Bayesian model averaging over local splines

statistics, GPs

Each candidate is a local model. Bayesian model averaging weights models by prior times evidence; WENO does the same with \(d_r\) as the prior and a power of roughness as the evidence:

\[ \mathbb{P}(r \mid \vect{q}) \propto \pi_r \mathbb{P}(\vect{q} \mid r) \ \longleftrightarrow \ \omega_r \propto d_r (\tilde\beta_r+\varepsilon)^{-p} . \]

The roughness \(\beta_r = \int (p_r')^2 \mathrm{d}\xi + \int (p_r'')^2 \mathrm{d}\xi\) is the same kind of penalty that smoothing splines minimise and that a Matérn Gaussian process uses as its norm. The difference is the shape of the evidence: WENO's is a power law, heavy-tailed, so a rough candidate is down-weighted but not exponentially suppressed. The Bayesian alternative is a Gibbs form with a temperature \(T\):

\[ \omega_r \propto d_r \exp(-\tilde\beta_r/T) . \]
Evidence\(\gvect{\omega}\), smooth\(\hat q\), smooth (exact 0.14944)\(\gvect{\omega}\), front
power law, \(p = 2\) (WENO5)(0.09, 0.64, 0.27)0.14915(0.14, 0.86, 0.00)
Gibbs, \(T = 0.1\)(0.08, 0.67, 0.25)0.14900(0.14, 0.86, 0.00)
Gibbs, \(T = 0.03\)(0.05, 0.81, 0.14)0.14825(0.14, 0.86, 0.00)

The weights are a posterior over "which local model is valid", so their entropy is a local model uncertainty. With WENO5's weights it is 0.85 on the smooth window and 0.41 on the front (the maximum, for three equal weights, is \(\log 3 = 1.10\)).

# Shapes: R = 3 candidates (candidate); H, W grid

# --- WENO: power-law evidence ---
# log ωᵣ = log dᵣ − p log(β̃ᵣ + ε) + const
log_evidence_power = -2.0 * jnp.log(beta_norm + eps)  # (R, H, W)

# --- Bayesian model averaging: exponential (Gibbs) evidence ---
# log p(v | r) = −β̃ᵣ / T + const, T a temperature
log_evidence_gibbs = -beta_norm / temperature  # (R, H, W)
# log ℙ(r | q) = log dᵣ + log ℙ(q | r) + const
log_posterior = einx.add(                   # (R,), (R, H, W) → (R, H, W)
    "candidate, candidate height width -> candidate height width",
    log_linear_weights,
    log_evidence_gibbs,
)
# ℙ(r | q) = softmax_r(log ℙ)
weights = einx.softmax("[candidate] height width", log_posterior)  # (R, H, W)

# --- model uncertainty per face ---
# log ωᵣ
log_weights = jnp.log(weights + 1e-30)      # (R, H, W)
# ωᵣ log ωᵣ
plogp = weights * log_weights               # (R, H, W)
# ℍ[ω] = −∑ᵣ ωᵣ log ωᵣ, high where no stencil is clearly right
entropy = -einx.sum("candidate height width -> height width", plogp)  # (H, W)

What transfers: a Gibbs evidence with a learned temperature is easier to calibrate than a power law. The entropy map is a state-dependent uncertainty that can feed the model-error covariance \(\gmat{\Sigma}_\eta\) on the assimilation page. Variants of WENO already replace the polynomial candidates with Gaussian-process predictions, scoring them with the GP likelihood.

4

Edge-preserving image filters

image processing, super-resolution

Reconstructing a face value from cell averages is sub-pixel interpolation, and ringing at a front is the same Gibbs problem as ringing at an image edge. Two image methods solve it with the same idea as WENO, in different ways. A bilateral filter weights neighbours by distance and by how different their values are, so it never averages across an edge:

\[ \hat q = \frac{\sum_j w_j q_j}{\sum_j w_j}, \] \[ w_j = \exp\left(-\frac{(x_j - x_{i+\frac12})^2}{2\sigma_\text{space}^2}\right) \exp\left(-\frac{(q_j - q_i)^2}{2\sigma_\text{range}^2}\right). \]

On the front window (with \(\sigma_\text{space} = \Delta x\), \(\sigma_\text{range} = 0.3\)) it gives 0.0006: the edge is respected. On the smooth window it gives 0.076 instead of 0.149, because its experts are constants: a local average cannot follow a slope. The right weights, the wrong experts.

Learned super-resolution of the RAISR type fixes that: it computes the local structure tensor \(\mat{M}_\text{struct} = \sum \nabla q \nabla q^\top\), hashes each pixel by edge orientation, strength and coherence, and applies a filter learned for that bucket:

\[ b = \mathrm{hash}(\theta_\text{dir}, \sigma_+^2, (\sigma_+^2 - \sigma_-^2)/(\sigma_+^2 + \sigma_-^2)), \] \[ \hat q = \langle\vect{c}_b, \text{patch}\rangle . \]

ENO is this with a fixed filter bank and a hash on \(\arg\min_r\beta_r\). Learned WENO is the soft version with the bank fixed. What WENO lacks is the orientation: it reconstructs one direction at a time, so a front oblique to the grid (the Gulf Stream, for instance) looks different to the x and y passes. Adding RAISR's features to the router gives it that information:

# Shapes: H, W grid (height, width); E = 6 WENO features (feature)

# --- gradient at cell centres ---
# today (finitevolx)
# ∂ₓq at U-points
grad_x_faces = difference.diff_x_T_to_U(q)  # (H, W) → (H, W)
# today (finitevolx)
# ∂ᵧq at V-points
grad_y_faces = difference.diff_y_T_to_V(q)  # (H, W) → (H, W)
# user
# average the two faces of each cell back to its centre
grad_x = faces_to_center_x(grad_x_faces)    # (H, W) → (H, W)
# user
grad_y = faces_to_center_y(grad_y_faces)    # (H, W) → (H, W)

# --- local structure tensor M_struct = ∑_{3×3} ∇q ∇qᵀ ---
# ∂ₓq², ∂ₓq ∂ᵧq, ∂ᵧq²
gxx = grad_x * grad_x                       # (H, W)
gxy = grad_x * grad_y                       # (H, W)
gyy = grad_y * grad_y                       # (H, W)
# user
# M_xx, M_xy, M_yy summed over the 3×3 neighbourhood
jxx, jxy, jyy = box_sum_3x3(gxx, gxy, gyy)  # (H, W) ×3

# --- RAISR-style features ---
# M_xx + M_yy = σ²₊ + σ²₋
trace = jxx + jyy                           # (H, W)
# M_xx − M_yy
diagonal_gap = jxx - jyy                    # (H, W)
# 2 M_xy
off_diagonal = 2.0 * jxy                    # (H, W)
# σ²₊ − σ²₋ = √((M_xx − M_yy)² + 4 M_xy²)
eigen_gap = jnp.hypot(diagonal_gap, off_diagonal)  # (H, W)
# coherence = (σ²₊ − σ²₋)/(σ²₊ + σ²₋): 1 for a straight front, 0 for no direction
coherence = eigen_gap / (trace + 1e-30)     # (H, W)
# 2θ_dir = atan2(2 M_xy, M_xx − M_yy): front orientation, sign-free
two_theta = jnp.arctan2(off_diagonal, diagonal_gap)  # (H, W)
# (cos 2θ_dir, sin 2θ_dir): continuous, no wrap at ±π
orient_cos = jnp.cos(two_theta)             # (H, W)
orient_sin = jnp.sin(two_theta)             # (H, W)

# --- add to the router's features ---
# e′ = (e, cos 2θ_dir, sin 2θ_dir, coherence), all scale-free
features_2d = einx.rearrange(               # → (E + 3, H, W)
    "feature height width, height width, height width, height width"
    " -> (feature + 1 + 1 + 1) height width",
    features,
    orient_cos,
    orient_sin,
    coherence,
)

What transfers: orientation and coherence as router features, for fronts oblique to the grid. And from the bilateral filter, the warning that data-dependent weights are not enough: the experts must be accurate enough on their own.

In one sentence: WENO is soft attention over a fixed bank of local polynomial experts, scored by their roughness. Learned WENO learns the score and keeps the experts.

Code sketch

The x-direction face flux of learned WENO5. WENO5_CANDIDATES holds the three candidate rows of the table above, padded to the five-point window; WENO5_BETA_MATRICES holds the three \(\mat{M}_r\). stencil_windows_x, candidate_wet_x and shift_east are short page-level helpers for the shifted views and the per-candidate wet test.

import einx
import equinox as eqx
import finitevolx as fvx
import jax
import jax.numpy as jnp
from jaxtyping import Array, Bool, Float, Int, PRNGKeyArray

# Shapes: H, W = 130 × 130 grid with ghost ring (height, width);
#         P = 5 stencil points q_{i−2} … q_{i+2} (point);
#         R = 3 candidate stencils (candidate); E = 6 features (feature)


class LearnedWENO5(eqx.Module):
    """WENO5 whose candidate weights get a learned, gated logit correction.

    ωᵣ = softmax_r( log dᵣ − 2 log(β̃ᵣ + ε) + s(τ̃) g_corr(features; θ)ᵣ ).

    Algorithm:
        1. Build the three 3-point candidate face values q̂ʳ.
        2. Score each candidate's smoothness βᵣ and normalise: β̃ᵣ = βᵣ / ∑β.
        3. Classical WENO5 logits, plus a learned correction gated by the
           WENO-Z smoothness detector τ̃, so smooth flow keeps WENO5 exactly.
        4. Zero the weight of any candidate that reads a dry cell.
        5. Blend: q̂ = ∑ᵣ ωᵣ q̂ʳ, and add a learned non-negative diffusivity.

    Args:
        weight_net: Pointwise network g_corr, features → R logit corrections.
        diffusivity_net: Pointwise network, features → one raw diffusivity.
        candidate_coeffs: Fixed cʳⱼ, padded to the 5-point window.
        beta_matrices: Fixed quadratic forms with βᵣ = qᵀ Mᵣ q.
        log_linear_weights: log dᵣ, with d = (0.1, 0.6, 0.3).
    """

    weight_net: eqx.nn.Conv2d
    diffusivity_net: eqx.nn.Conv2d
    candidate_coeffs: Float[Array, "candidate point"]
    beta_matrices: Float[Array, "candidate point point"]
    log_linear_weights: Float[Array, "candidate"]
    eps_rel: float = 1e-6

    def __init__(self, *, key: PRNGKeyArray):
        weight_key, diffusivity_key = jax.random.split(key)
        # user
        weight_net = eqx.nn.Conv2d(6, 3, kernel_size=1, key=weight_key)
        # zero last layer: g_corr ≡ 0 at init, so training starts from WENO5
        self.weight_net = eqx.tree_at(
            lambda net: net.weight, weight_net, jnp.zeros_like(weight_net.weight)
        )
        # user
        self.diffusivity_net = eqx.nn.Conv2d(6, 1, kernel_size=1, key=diffusivity_key)
        self.candidate_coeffs = WENO5_CANDIDATES
        self.beta_matrices = WENO5_BETA_MATRICES
        self.log_linear_weights = jnp.log(jnp.array([0.1, 0.6, 0.3]))

    def face_value(
        self,
        window: Float[Array, "point height width"],
        courant: Float[Array, "height width"],
        wet_run: Int[Array, "2 height width"],
        candidate_wet: Bool[Array, "candidate height width"],
    ) -> tuple[Float[Array, "height width"], Float[Array, "height width"]]:
        # --- candidates ---
        # q̂ʳ = ∑ⱼ cʳⱼ q_{i+j}
        candidates = einx.dot(                        # (R, P), (P, H, W) → (R, H, W)
            "candidate point, point height width -> candidate height width",
            self.candidate_coeffs,
            window,
        )

        # --- smoothness ---
        # βᵣ = qᵀ Mᵣ q
        betas = einx.dot(                             # → (R, H, W)
            "candidate p1 p2, p1 height width, p2 height width"
            " -> candidate height width",
            self.beta_matrices,
            window,
            window,
        )
        # ∑β, held out of the gradient: the weights depend on ratios only
        beta_sum = einx.sum(                          # (R, H, W) → (H, W)
            "candidate height width -> height width", betas
        )
        beta_sum = jax.lax.stop_gradient(beta_sum)    # (H, W)
        # ∑β + ε_tiny, so a flat window gives the linear weights
        beta_sum = beta_sum + 1e-30                   # (H, W)
        # β̃ᵣ = βᵣ / ∑β
        betas_normalised = einx.divide(               # (R, H, W), (H, W) → (R, H, W)
            "candidate height width, height width -> candidate height width",
            betas,
            beta_sum,
        )
        # τ̃ = |β̃₁ − β̃₃|, the WENO-Z detector: ≈ 0 where the field is smooth
        beta_gap = betas_normalised[0] - betas_normalised[2]  # → (H, W)
        detector = jnp.abs(beta_gap)                  # (H, W)

        # --- features ---
        # e = (β̃₁, β̃₂, β̃₃, C, n⁻, n⁺), all scale-free
        wet_run_float = wet_run.astype(window.dtype)  # (2, H, W)
        features = einx.rearrange(                    # → (E, H, W)
            "candidate height width, height width, two height width"
            " -> (candidate + 1 + two) height width",
            betas_normalised,
            courant,
            wet_run_float,
        )

        # --- weights ---
        # β̃ᵣ + ε
        shifted_beta = betas_normalised + self.eps_rel  # (R, H, W)
        # log(β̃ᵣ + ε)
        log_beta = jnp.log(shifted_beta)              # (R, H, W)
        # 2 log(β̃ᵣ + ε)
        log_beta_twice = 2.0 * log_beta               # (R, H, W)
        # log αᵣ = log dᵣ − 2 log(β̃ᵣ + ε)
        classic_logits = einx.subtract(               # (R,), (R, H, W) → (R, H, W)
            "candidate, candidate height width -> candidate height width",
            self.log_linear_weights,
            log_beta_twice,
        )
        # user
        # g_corr(e; θ)
        correction = self.weight_net(features)        # (E, H, W) → (R, H, W)
        # s(τ̃) g_corr(e; θ), gated off where the field is smooth
        gated = einx.multiply(                        # → (R, H, W)
            "height width, candidate height width -> candidate height width",
            detector,
            correction,
        )
        logits = classic_logits + gated               # (R, H, W)
        # candidates that read a dry cell get logit −∞
        logits = jnp.where(candidate_wet, logits, -1e30)  # (R, H, W)
        # ωᵣ = softmax_r(logits)
        weights = einx.softmax("[candidate] height width", logits)  # (R, H, W)
        # q̂ = ∑ᵣ ωᵣ q̂ʳ
        face = einx.dot(                              # (R, H, W), (R, H, W) → (H, W)
            "candidate height width, candidate height width -> height width",
            weights,
            candidates,
        )

        # --- extra dissipation ---
        # user
        raw = self.diffusivity_net(features)          # (E, H, W) → (1, H, W)
        # κ = softplus(raw) ≥ 0
        kappa = jax.nn.softplus(raw[0])               # (H, W)
        return face, kappa

    def flux_x(
        self,
        q: Float[Array, "height width"],
        u: Float[Array, "height width"],
        courant: Float[Array, "height width"],
        mask: fvx.Mask2D,
    ) -> Float[Array, "height width"]:
        capability = mask.stencil_capability
        # user
        # windows q_{i−2…i+2} (u ≥ 0) and q_{i+3…i−1} (u < 0, mirrored)
        window_pos, window_neg = stencil_windows_x(q)  # (H, W) → (P, H, W) ×2
        # user
        wet_pos, wet_neg = candidate_wet_x(capability)  # → (R, H, W) ×2
        # today (finitevolx)
        # n⁻, n⁺: contiguous wet cells upstream and downstream
        runs_pos = einx.rearrange(                    # (H, W) ×2 → (2, H, W)
            "height width, height width -> (1 + 1) height width",
            capability.x_neg,
            capability.x_pos,
        )
        runs_neg = einx.rearrange(                    # (H, W) ×2 → (2, H, W)
            "height width, height width -> (1 + 1) height width",
            capability.x_pos,
            capability.x_neg,
        )

        # same network both ways: no directional bias
        face_pos, kappa_pos = self.face_value(window_pos, courant, runs_pos, wet_pos)
        face_neg, kappa_neg = self.face_value(window_neg, courant, runs_neg, wet_neg)

        # q̂_{i+½} from the upwind side
        upwind = u >= 0.0                             # (H, W)
        face = jnp.where(upwind, face_pos, face_neg)  # (H, W)
        kappa = jnp.where(upwind, kappa_pos, kappa_neg)  # (H, W)

        # user
        # q_{i+1}
        q_east = shift_east(q)                        # (H, W) → (H, W)
        # Δq_{i+½} = q_{i+1} − q_i
        jump = q_east - q                             # (H, W)
        # F = u q̂ − |u| κ (q_{i+1} − q_i)
        advective = u * face                          # (H, W)
        diffusive = jnp.abs(u) * kappa * jump         # (H, W)
        flux = advective - diffusive                  # (H, W)
        # dry faces carry no flux
        flux = jnp.where(mask.u, flux, 0.0)           # (H, W)
        return flux


# today (finitevolx)
# the g_corr ≡ 0 oracle: classical masked WENO5 on the same grid and mask
baseline = fvx.Advection2D(grid, mask=mask)(pv, u, v, method="weno5")  # (H, W)

Where it goes in the QG model

The model on the other pages advects PV with the Arakawa Jacobian. Two routes:

RouteAdvectionDissipationWhen
(a) keep Arakawa\(-J(\psi,q)\): conserves energy and enstrophy exactly, no implicit dissipationall explicit: replace \(\nu\nabla^6\psi\) with a learned, sign-definite closurefirst; smallest change
(b) learned WENOflux form \(-\nabla\cdot(\vect{v} q)\) with \(u = -\partial_y\psi\), \(v = \partial_x\psi\) on the C-gridimplicit in the weights, plus \(\kappa_{\gvect{\theta}}\); drop \(\nu\nabla^6\psi\)when fronts dominate; and for shallow-water or primitive equations, where Arakawa's guarantees do not carry over

Because the discrete velocity comes from a streamfunction, it has zero discrete divergence, and the flux form and the advective form agree. Route (b) changes the reconstruction, not the physics.

Training

  • Target. A QG run at \(\Delta x = 2\) km, coarse-grained to 8 km by cell averaging. This is a correction target in the taxonomy's terms: the scheme learns what the coarse grid misses near fronts.
  • Loss. A posteriori, through the solver: rollout error of \(q\) over several days, plus the error of the kinetic energy spectrum at high wavenumbers, so the scheme is judged on the scales it controls.
  • Start. From classical WENO5 (zero last layer), so every gradient step starts from a working, stable scheme.
  • Check of what it learned. Map \(\omega\) and \(\kappa_{\gvect{\theta}}\) over a snapshot. They are physical diagnostics: where the scheme trusts the upwind side, and where it dissipates.

For data assimilation

  • A true adjoint. Classical limiters use min, max and switches, whose adjoints are discontinuous; 4D-Var systems often swap in a smoother linearised scheme for the tangent-linear model. Here every weight is a smooth function of \(q\), so jax.grad of the forecast is the exact adjoint of the forecast that is run.
  • Information over the window. Numerical diffusion also damps perturbations in the tangent-linear model. Over a 10-day window, a too-diffusive scheme makes day-10 maps nearly uninformative about day 0. Lower, targeted dissipation keeps that information, so the advection scheme sets how long a window can usefully be.
  • A dissipation map as a control. \(\kappa_{\gvect{\theta}}\) (or a spatial scale on it) can be estimated from observations as a hyperparameter, the A1 route of the assimilation page.

What to watch

Learned forcing and learned dissipation are not identifiable together

A forcing that injects energy and a scheme that removes it can fit the same trajectories at any level of both. Constrain one: restrict the learned forcing to large scales (a smooth basis), keep \(\kappa_{\gvect{\theta}}\) and the weight correction at grid scale, or fit the scheme on free-decay runs before learning the forcing.

On a coarse grid, numerics and closure blur

A scheme trained against a finer run learns some subgrid physics as well as better numerics. That is fine, but test it on a grid spacing it was not trained on: if it only fixed numerics it should still help; if it learned a closure tied to 8 km it may not.

Order is measured, not guaranteed

The \(\tilde\tau\) gate makes the correction small in smooth flow, not zero. If the convergence test shows order loss, sharpen the gate (\(\tilde\tau^2\), or a threshold) before anything else.
