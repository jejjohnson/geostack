Variational data assimilation, written as probabilistic programs

3D-Var, strong-constraint 4D-Var and weak-constraint 4D-Var, each in a full and a reduced form, are one idea at six sizes, and incremental 4D-Var solves the strong-constraint pair a second way: a Gaussian prior on whatever is uncertain, a model that maps it to observations, and the most probable value given the data. This page writes each as a numpyro model whose covariances are gaussx operators (circulant, Kronecker, low-rank) built from kernellib kernels, with reduced bases from kernellib feature maps and geonnax eigenfunctions. The cost function is then derived, not hand-written; the analysis is the model's MAP; the Laplace approximation is its uncertainty.

TL;DR

Six priors, one likelihood, two ways to solve

  • A variational method is a choice of control and prior. 3D-Var: one state. Strong 4D-Var: the initial state. Weak: the initial state and every model error. Each has a reduced form that keeps only a few coefficients of the correction (of the state, of the initial state, or of the model error).
  • Each is a short numpyro model. Latent sites are the controls; the rollout is a deterministic site; the observations are one masked Gaussian site. numpyro's potential energy is the 4D-Var cost plus a constant.
  • The covariances are structured gaussx operators, and their square roots keep the structure: gx.circulant for a stationary \(\mathbf B\) (FFT), gx.Kronecker for a space–time \(\mathbf Q\), gx.low_rank_plus_diag for a reduced \(\mathbf B\) (Woodbury).
  • Sample whitened, not in physical units. The control-variable transform is a reparameterisation: on 3D-Var it cuts the optimiser from 1,952 to 27 iterations per analysis, for the same analysis (they differ by at most 0.0014).
  • On Lorenz-96 with a biased model the window error goes from 0.64 (strong) to 0.27 (weak) to 0.12 (one bias coefficient, recovered as 1.00 ± 0.04; truth 1.0). Long windows have many minima: solve them quasi-statically.

Intuition: one ladder

The six priors come in three pairs, each a full and a reduced version: 3D-Var, strong-constraint 4D-Var and weak-constraint 4D-Var. Incremental 4D-Var (rungs 5 and 6) adds no new prior: it solves the strong-constraint pair by Gauss–Newton. Every one answers the same question: which unknowns, a priori how large, best explain the observations? They differ only in what is unknown. Going down the ladder, the model is trusted less and the data are asked to explain more.

Written as a probabilistic program, the change from one rung to the next is a change of a few lines: which variables are numpyro.sample sites, and which covariance object their prior uses. Everything downstream (the cost, its gradient, the minimiser, the uncertainty) is shared.

The running example is the weak-constraint page's: Lorenz-96 with \(N = 40\) sites, truth forcing \(F = 8\), model forcing \(F = 7\) (a biased model), \(T = 20\) observation times of 10 RK4 steps, every second site observed with \(\sigma_o = 0.5\), and a Gaussian-correlated background error (\(\sigma_b = 1\), length 2 sites).

The program behind every method

Collect a method's controls, whitened, in \(v\). Each method is a joint density

\[ p(v, y) = \underbrace{p(v)}_{\text{prior: }\mathbf B,\ \mathbf Q,\ \text{bases}}\ \prod_{k=1}^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{M}\ \underbrace{\mathcal N\big(y_{ki};\ (\mathbf H z_k(v))_i,\ \sigma_o^2\big)^{m_{ki}}}_{\text{likelihood, masked}}, \]

where \(m_{ki}\in\{0, 1\}\) switches observation \(i\) at time \(k\) on or off, and \(z_k(v)\) is whatever the method's dynamics make of the controls: the model run from \(z_0\), with or without kicks \(\eta_k\). numpyro evaluates \(U(v) = -\log p(v, y)\), its potential energy. For Gaussian priors and likelihoods, \(U = \mathcal J + \text{const}\): the variational cost appears without being written. The analysis is the MAP, \(v^\star = \arg\min U\). The Laplace approximation \(\mathcal N(v^\star, \mathbf A^{-1})\), with \(\mathbf A\) the Hessian of \(U\) at \(v^\star\) (or its Gauss–Newton part), is the posterior spread.

Every prior here is Gaussian, and in the main models every one is sampled whitened (the readable 3D-Var, the low-rank model and the state formulation are kept in physical units for comparison): a standard-normal site \(\xi\) mapped through a square root, \(z_0 = z_b + \mathbf B^{1/2}\xi\). This is the control-variable transform of operational 4D-Var, written as a numpyro reparameterisation. The posterior is unchanged; the optimiser sees an identity-conditioned prior instead of \(\mathbf B^{-1}\), whose condition number here is about \(10^{7}\).

The machinery: priors as structured objects

Three structures cover all eight methods. A stationary covariance on a periodic grid is circulant: the FFT diagonalises it, so its solve, log-determinant and square root are elementwise in Fourier space. A space–time covariance that separates is a Kronecker product: its square root is the Kronecker product of the factors' square roots, never a \(TN\times TN\) matrix. And a stationary covariance has a Mercer expansion: a few weighted Fourier modes reproduce most of it, which gives a reduced control and its prior in one step.

kernellib supplies the kernels. \(\mathbf B\) uses its periodic kernel with \(\ell_p = 2\pi\ell/N\), the ring version of a Gaussian correlation of \(\ell = 2\) sites; its first column, put into gx.circulant, is the whole operator. The reduced methods use kl.PeriodicFeatures, the exact truncated Fourier series of that kernel: \(K\) harmonics give \(R = 2K + 1\) features \(G\), already weighted so that \(\mathbf B\approx GG^\top\).

def three_dvar_readable(
    forecast: Float[Array, "state"],
    prior_cov: lx.AbstractLinearOperator,
    obs_index: Int[Array, "obs"],
    obs_std: float,
    obs: Float[Array, "obs"] | None = None,
    obs_mask: Bool[Array, "obs"] | bool = True,
):
    # today (gaussx)
    # z ~ 𝒩(z_f, B)
    state = numpyro.sample(                                 # event (N,)
        "state", gx.MultivariateNormal(forecast, prior_cov)
    )
    # y ~ 𝒩(H z, σ_o² I), masked where m = 0
    likelihood = dist.Normal(state[obs_index], obs_std)     # batch (M,)
    numpyro.sample("obs", likelihood.mask(obs_mask).to_event(1), obs=obs)

def rollout(model_step, initial, errors=None, num_times=None):
    """z₁:T from z₀, with an optional kick η_k after every interval."""

    def interval(state, error):
        # user (model_step: one observation interval of the dynamics)
        # z_k = 𝓜_k(z_{k−1}) (+ η_k)
        state = model_step(state) if error is None else model_step(state) + error
        return state, state

    _, states = jax.lax.scan(interval, initial, errors, length=num_times)  # → (T, N)
    return states


def strong_4dvar(
    model_step: Callable,
    background: Float[Array, "state"],
    prior_sqrt: lx.AbstractLinearOperator,
    num_times: int,
    obs_index: Int[Array, "obs"],
    obs_std: float,
    obs: Float[Array, "time obs"] | None = None,
    obs_mask: Bool[Array, "time obs"] | bool = True,
):
    # ξ ~ 𝒩(0, I)
    whitened = numpyro.sample(                              # event (N,)
        "whitened", dist.Normal(0.0, 1.0).expand(background.shape).to_event(1)
    )
    # z₀ = z_b + B^{1/2} ξ
    initial = numpyro.deterministic(                        # (N,)
        "initial", background + prior_sqrt.mv(whitened)
    )
    # z_k = 𝓜_{k←0}(z₀): deterministic, so the posterior lives on ξ alone
    states = numpyro.deterministic(                         # (T, N)
        "states", rollout(model_step, initial, num_times=num_times)
    )
    # y_k ~ 𝒩(H z_k, σ_o² I) for every k
    likelihood = dist.Normal(states[:, obs_index], obs_std)  # batch (T, M)
    numpyro.sample("obs", likelihood.mask(obs_mask).to_event(1), obs=obs)

The Laplace spread of \(z_0\) is 0.12, but its actual error is 0.85: the posterior is confident and wrong, because the model it trusts is biased. A ratio of error to spread far above 1 is the same alarm as \(2\mathcal J^\star/p\gg 1\).

5 · Incremental 4D-Var

Intuition. Strong-constraint 4D-Var is hard because the model is nonlinear. Incremental 4D-Var takes that difficulty out of the inner solve: run the nonlinear model once from the current analysis, linearise it around that run, and solve the resulting linear-Gaussian problem exactly. Then rerun the model from the new analysis and repeat. It is Gauss–Newton on the strong-constraint cost: it targets the same MAP, and its Laplace spread uses the Gauss–Newton Hessian. In numpyro terms, the inner problem is the strong-constraint program with the rollout replaced by its tangent-linear approximation, which jax.linearize provides.

\[ \begin{aligned} z_0^r &= z_b + \mathbf B^{1/2}\xi^r \\ z_k &\approx z_k^r + \mathbf M'_{k\leftarrow0}(z_0 - z_0^r) \\ \mathcal J_{\text{inner}}(\xi) &= \tfrac12\|\xi\|^2 + \tfrac12\sum_k\big\|y_k - \mathbf H z_k^r - \mathbf H\mathbf M'_{k\leftarrow0}\mathbf B^{1/2}(\xi - \xi^r)\big\|^2_{\sigma_o^{-2}} \\ \xi^{r+1} &= \xi^r + \alpha\,(\xi^\star - \xi^r), \quad \alpha\in\{1, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, \dots\} \end{aligned} \]

The inner cost is quadratic in \(\xi\), so the linearised problem's posterior is exactly Gaussian and its Laplace approximation is exact; for the nonlinear problem it is the Gauss–Newton approximation at the analysis. The step length \(\alpha\) is halved until the nonlinear cost decreases: on this chaotic window, plain Gauss–Newton steps overshoot. Without the halving, the cost over the last four outer loops went 514.2 → 511.0 → 512.0 → 512.2 for the full increment and 1362 → 1413 → 1371 → 1442 with 9 features.

def incremental_inner(
    reference_initial: Float[Array, "state"],
    reference_states: Float[Array, "time state"],
    tangent: Callable,
    initial_of: Callable,
    num_controls: int,
    obs_index: Int[Array, "obs"],
    obs_std: float,
    obs: Float[Array, "time obs"] | None = None,
    obs_mask: Bool[Array, "time obs"] | bool = True,
):
    """The inner problem: the strong-constraint program with the model linearised."""
    # u ~ 𝒩(0, I): ξ for the full increment, u for the reduced one
    whitened = numpyro.sample(                              # event (D,)
        "whitened", dist.Normal(0.0, 1.0).expand([num_controls]).to_event(1)
    )
    # z₀ = z_b + B^{1/2} ξ, or z_b + G u
    initial = numpyro.deterministic("initial", initial_of(whitened))  # (N,)
    # δz₀ = z₀ − z₀ʳ, the departure from the outer loop's reference
    increment = initial - reference_initial                 # (N,)
    # z_k ≈ z_kʳ + M′_{k←0} δz₀: tangent-linear, so z is linear in u
    states = numpyro.deterministic(                         # (T, N)
        "states", reference_states + tangent(increment)
    )
    # y_k ~ 𝒩(H z_k, σ_o² I): linear-Gaussian, so the posterior is Gaussian
    likelihood = dist.Normal(states[:, obs_index], obs_std)  # batch (T, M)
    numpyro.sample("obs", likelihood.mask(obs_mask).to_event(1), obs=obs)


def nonlinear_cost(model_step, initial_of, whitened, num_times, obs_index, obs_std,
                   obs, obs_mask=True):
    """𝒥(u) = ½‖u‖² + ½ Σ_k ‖y_k − H 𝓜_{k←0}(z₀(u))‖²/σ_o², the outer-loop cost."""
    states = rollout(model_step, initial_of(whitened), num_times=num_times)  # (T, N)
    # r_k = (y_k − H z_k) / σ_o, zero where m = 0
    residual = (obs - states[:, obs_index]) / obs_std       # (T, M)
    residual = jnp.where(obs_mask, residual, 0.0)           # (T, M)
    return 0.5 * jnp.sum(whitened**2) + 0.5 * jnp.sum(residual**2)  # → ()


def incremental_4dvar(
    model_step: Callable,
    initial_of: Callable,
    num_controls: int,
    num_times: int,
    num_outer: int,
    obs_index: Int[Array, "obs"],
    obs_std: float,
    obs: Float[Array, "time obs"],
    obs_mask: Bool[Array, "time obs"] | bool = True,
    init_values: dict | None = None,
):
    """Gauss–Newton: relinearise, solve the linear-Gaussian inner program, repeat."""
    values = init_values or {"whitened": jnp.zeros(num_controls)}
    data = (num_times, obs_index, obs_std, obs, obs_mask)
    cost = nonlinear_cost(model_step, initial_of, values["whitened"], *data)
    inner_steps = 0
    for _ in range(num_outer):
        # z₀ʳ, the current analysis
        reference_initial = initial_of(values["whitened"])  # (N,)

        def trajectory(initial):
            return rollout(model_step, initial, num_times=num_times)  # (N,) → (T, N)

        # z₁:Tʳ = 𝓜(z₀ʳ) and δz₀ ↦ M′ δz₀: one nonlinear run per outer loop
        reference_states, tangent = jax.linearize(trajectory, reference_initial)
        inner, solution, potential_fn, unravel = fit_map(
            incremental_inner,
            values,
            reference_initial,
            reference_states,
            tangent,
            initial_of,
            num_controls,
            obs_index,
            obs_std,
            obs=obs,
            obs_mask=obs_mask,
        )
        inner_steps += int(solution.stats["num_steps"])
        # Δu, the Gauss–Newton step
        step = inner["whitened"] - values["whitened"]       # (D,)
        # backtrack: halve Δu until the nonlinear 𝒥 decreases
        for _ in range(6):
            trial = values["whitened"] + step               # (D,)
            trial_cost = nonlinear_cost(model_step, initial_of, trial, *data)
            if trial_cost < cost:
                values, cost = {"whitened": trial}, trial_cost
                break
            step = 0.5 * step                               # (D,)
    return values, cost, inner_steps

    model_step: Callable,
    background: Float[Array, "state"],
    prior_sqrt: lx.AbstractLinearOperator,
    spatial_basis: Float[Array, "state basis"],
    temporal_basis: Float[Array, "time temporal"],
    coeff_sqrt: lx.AbstractLinearOperator,
    num_times: int,
    obs_index: Int[Array, "obs"],
    obs_std: float,
    obs: Float[Array, "time obs"] | None = None,
    obs_mask: Bool[Array, "time obs"] | bool = True,
):

    num_spatial = spatial_basis.shape[1]
    whitened = numpyro.sample(                              # event (N,)
        "whitened", dist.Normal(0.0, 1.0).expand(background.shape).to_event(1)
    )
    initial = numpyro.deterministic(                        # (N,)
        "initial", background + prior_sqrt.mv(whitened)
    )

    whitened_coeffs = numpyro.sample(                       # event (C·P,)

        dist.Normal(0.0, 1.0).expand([num_temporal * num_spatial]).to_event(1),
    )
    # vec_r W = (Λ_t ⊗ Λ_s)^{1/2} ω
    stacked_coeffs = coeff_sqrt.mv(whitened_coeffs)         # (C·P,)
    coeffs = einx.id("(c p) -> c p", stacked_coeffs, c=num_temporal)  # → (C, P)
    numpyro.deterministic("coeffs", coeffs)
    # η(x_n, t_k) = Σ_c Σ_j χ_c(t_k) W_cj φ_j(x_n)
    # (T, C), (C, P), (N, P) → (T, N)

        "t c, c p, n p -> t n", temporal_basis, coeffs, spatial_basis
    )

        "states", rollout(model_step, initial, errors)
    )

Inference: MAP, quasi-static windows, Laplace

One routine fits all eight models (for the incremental ones, each inner problem). numpyro's initialize_model returns the potential energy over the flattened latent sites; L-BFGS minimises it. The mask in the likelihood does double duty: it marks missing observations, and it lets the window grow a few observation times at a time.

import jax
import optimistix as optx
from jax.flatten_util import ravel_pytree
from numpyro.infer.util import init_to_value, initialize_model

# Shapes: D latent values in total (latent); T = 20 observation times (time)


def fit_map(model, init_values, *args, **kwargs):
    """The variational analysis: the mode of the model's posterior."""
    # today (numpyro)
    # U(v) = −log p(v, y), the potential energy: 𝒥(v) + const
    info = initialize_model(
        jax.random.key(0),
        model,
        model_args=args,
        model_kwargs=kwargs,
        init_strategy=init_to_value(values=init_values),
    )
    # v: every latent site, flattened
    flat_init, unravel = ravel_pytree(info.param_info.z)    # (D,)

    def potential(flat, _):
        return info.potential_fn(unravel(flat))             # (D,) → ()

    # not pinned (optimistix)
    # v* = argmin U(v)
    solution = optx.minimise(
        potential,
        optx.LBFGS(rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-6),
        flat_init,
        max_steps=20_000,
        throw=False,
    )
    mode = unravel(solution.value)
    return mode, solution, info.potential_fn, unravel


def quasi_static(model, init_values, *args, num_times, stride=2, **kwargs):
    """Lengthen the window a few observation times at a time, warm-started."""
    values = init_values
    for window in range(stride, num_times + 1, stride):
        # m_k = 1 for k < window, so later observations are switched off
        in_window = jnp.arange(num_times) < window          # (T,)
        obs_mask = einx.id("t -> t 1", in_window)           # (T,) → (T, 1)
        values, solution, potential_fn, unravel = fit_map(
            model, values, *args, obs_mask=obs_mask, **kwargs
        )
    return values, solution, potential_fn, unravel


def laplace(solution, potential_fn, unravel):
    """𝒩(v*, A⁻¹): the posterior spread at the analysis."""
    # A = ∇² U(v*), the posterior precision, dense: for small D only
    hessian = jax.hessian(                                  # (D, D)
        lambda flat: potential_fn(unravel(flat))
    )(solution.value)
    # today (lineax)
    precision = lx.MatrixLinearOperator(                    # (D, D) PSD op
        hessian, lx.positive_semidefinite_tag
    )
    # today (gaussx)
    # p(v | y) ≈ 𝒩(v*, A⁻¹), stored by its precision A
    return gx.MultivariateNormalPrecision(solution.value, precision)  # event (D,)

Why quasi-static. A 20-interval Lorenz-96 window spans several error-doubling times, and the strong-constraint cost has many minima. From six starting points, L-BFGS found 6 different minima, with \(\mathcal J^\star\) from 1,455 to 4,793 and window errors from 1.50 to 2.56; on the first 10 intervals all six reached the same minimum (\(\mathcal J^\star = \) 140.5). Lengthening the window two observation times at a time, warm-starting each stage, found a deeper minimum than any of the six (\(\mathcal J^\star = \) 509, window error 0.64). It is a heuristic, not a guarantee. Every 4D-Var analysis on this page uses it, except the state formulation (which converged without it), the no-schedule incremental row and the multistart experiment itself.

Laplace at scale. The dense Hessian above is fine for 40 controls and affordable for 840. At ocean size, use the Gauss–Newton precision \(\mathbf I + \mathbf J_r^\top\mathbf J_r\), which gaussx keeps as a low-rank update of the identity: Woodbury solves and log-determinants of the size of the observation vector. Drawing samples from it still factors the precision densely in gaussx today. On the weak-constraint analysis (840 controls, 400 observations) the dense Hessian took about 10 s; the Gauss–Newton marginal standard deviations were within 1% of it at every control.

Notes from building this page

  • gaussx: MultivariateNormal, MultivariateNormalPrecision, circulant, Kronecker, sqrt, low_rank_plus_diag and gauss_newton_precision all worked as documented. The square root of a low_rank_plus_diag falls back to a dense matrix, which is why the low-rank model keeps an unwhitened MVN site; gaussx has a structured root only for orthonormal factors on a scaled identity (low_rank_plus_identity(..., orthonormal=True)), and these features are orthogonal but not normalised. A gaussx MVN site inside a numpyro scan failed with a shape error, so the state formulation uses numpyro's Normal.
  • kernellib: kl.to_operator returns a dense Gram for a periodic kernel on a regular ring; it does not detect the circulant structure, so the page builds the first column and calls gx.circulant. kl.PeriodicFeatures and spectral_density are public and matched the direct computation.
  • pyrox: the same spectral density is importable only from the private pyrox_gp._basis; HSGPFeatures offers only Dirichlet boundaries.
  • The weak-constraint page's strong-constraint numbers are a local minimum. It reported \(\mathcal J^\star = 1519\) (\(2\mathcal J^\star/p = 7.6\)) and a window error of 1.66; with this page's periodic \(\mathbf B\), the quasi-static solve finds \(\mathcal J^\star = \) 509 (2.55) and 0.64. The conclusion stands (the strong analysis cannot fit a biased model), with smaller margins.
