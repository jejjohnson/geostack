Ocean state estimation, from the generative story to each box

The traditional formulation of estimating the ocean state from sea-level maps, written once, then read box by box. Steps 1 and 2 are the classical problem. Steps 3 to 6 are what that problem turns into when parts of it are learned.

TL;DR

Every box is an operator

Before any equation, fix what goes in and what comes out. For this problem the answer is short:

  • Forward boxes (1, 3) map a sea-level field and two physical parameters to the next field. Box 1 chains that map into a cube of states and a cube of synthetic maps.
  • Inverse boxes (2, 4, 5) all have one signature: a cube of daily SSH maps with their masks and errors in, a cube of states plus the parameters and an uncertainty out. They differ only in what is inside and in what each query costs.
  • Box 6 takes many windows of those outputs and returns diagnostics and one decision: rerun the inner loop, or change the model.

So the task picks the signature, and the box picks the implementation. Swapping box 2 for box 5 changes no wiring around it.

The problem and the data

Take a box in the Gulf Stream extension, about 1000 km on a side, centred near 40°N. Satellite altimetry has already been gridded into daily maps of sea surface height: that was the L2 → L3 step, optimal interpolation from along-track data to a map with an error estimate. What we want is the L4 product: the full dynamical ocean state over a window of days, consistent with the physics and with the maps. Run forward in real time, this is an analysis and forecast. Run backward over a whole window, it is a reanalysis.

The difference from L2 → L3 is the model. An optimal-interpolation map uses only a covariance. A state estimate uses a dynamical model that carries information between days, so a feature seen on Monday constrains the field on Thursday.

Two clashes with DA vocabulary

DA papers call the variable 4D-Var optimises the "control vector". Here that is \((\mathbf z_0,\theta)\), not \(\mathbf u\); \(\mathbf u\) is forcing we are given. And DA papers write the state as \(\mathbf x\); here \(\mathbf x\) is reserved for covariates.

The mathematical objects

Every symbol below is used in the rest of the page. Index \(k = 0,1,\dots,K\) counts observation times \(t_k\), one per day.

State

The state \(\mathbf z_k \in \mathbb R^{N}\) is sea surface height \(\eta\) on the grid at time \(t_k\), flattened to a vector. In the quasi-geostrophic (QG) model the streamfunction and potential vorticity are diagnosed from it, so \(\eta\) alone is a complete state.

Dynamics: the 1.5-layer QG model

Let \(g\) be gravity, \(\psi\) the streamfunction, \(q\) the potential vorticity, \(\nabla^2\) the horizontal Laplacian, \(J(a,b) = \partial_x a\,\partial_y b - \partial_y a\,\partial_x b\) the Jacobian (advection of \(b\) by the flow of \(a\)), and \(\nu\) a biharmonic viscosity. Then

\[ \psi = \frac{g}{f_0}\,\eta, \qquad q = \nabla^2\psi - \frac{\psi}{L_d^2} + \beta y, \qquad \partial_t q + J(\psi, q) = -\nu\,\nabla^6\psi + u . \]

One time step advects \(q\) (Arakawa Jacobian), then inverts the Helmholtz operator \(\nabla^2 - L_d^{-2}\) to recover \(\psi\), hence \(\eta\). The forcing \(u = \nabla\times\boldsymbol\tau/(\rho_0 H)\) is the wind-stress curl from an atmospheric reanalysis, with \(\rho_0\) the reference density and \(H\) the layer depth; it is a control \(\mathbf u_k\), given for each day. The planetary vorticity \(\beta y\) and the grid coordinates are covariates \(\mathbf x\). Collect the physical parameters in \(\theta = (L_d, \nu)\). Integrating 48 steps from \(t_{k-1}\) to \(t_k\) defines the model operator \(\mathcal F_\theta : \mathbb R^N \to \mathbb R^N\) and its composition \(\mathcal F^{(k)}_\theta\) from \(t_0\) to \(t_k\):

\[ \mathbf z_k = \mathcal F_\theta(\mathbf z_{k-1};\,\mathbf x,\mathbf u_k) + \mathbf w_k, \qquad \mathcal F^{(k)}_\theta = \underbrace{\mathcal F_\theta\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal F_\theta}_{k\ \text{times}} . \]

Here \(\mathbf w_k \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf Q)\) is model error, with covariance \(\mathbf Q \in \mathbb R^{N\times N}\). Its Jacobian \(\mathbf F_k = \partial \mathcal F_\theta / \partial \mathbf z\,|_{\mathbf z_{k-1}}\) is the tangent-linear model; \(\mathbf F_k^\top\) is the adjoint.

Observations

The map on day \(k\) has \(p_k \le N\) usable pixels (land, ice and high-error pixels are removed). Write \(\mathbf y_k \in \mathbb R^{p_k}\) for those pixels and \(\mathbf H_k \in \{0,1\}^{p_k\times N}\) for the selection matrix that picks them out of the state:

\[ \mathbf y_k = \mathbf H_k \mathbf z_k + \boldsymbol\varepsilon_k, \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon_k \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf R_k). \]

\(\mathbf R_k \in \mathbb R^{p_k\times p_k}\) is the observation-error covariance. For a gridded map it is the mapping error from the L2 → L3 optimal interpolation, plus representation error (the QG model has no tides, no steric seasonal cycle).

Watch out

Assimilating maps instead of tracks is convenient but not innocent. The map's errors are spatially correlated, so a diagonal \(\mathbf R_k\) overweights the data, and the map already contains a prior from the L2 → L3 step that is counted again here. The cleaner traditional choice is to assimilate the L2 along-track heights directly, with \(\mathbf H_k\) an interpolation from the grid to the track points. The algebra below is identical in both cases.

Prior

The background \(\mathbf z_b\) is our best guess of \(\mathbf z_0\) before seeing this window, typically the previous window's analysis. Its error covariance \(\mathbf B \in \mathbb R^{N\times N}\) is usually a stationary kernel, for example Matérn with a standard deviation of 10 cm and a length scale of 100 km. The parameters get their own prior \(\theta \sim \mathcal N(\theta_b, \boldsymbol\Sigma_\theta)\). Everything that shapes these distributions but is not itself estimated in a window (\(\sigma_b, \ell_b, \theta_b, \boldsymbol\Sigma_\theta\), the scales of \(\mathbf R\) and \(\mathbf Q\)) is a hyperparameter, collected in \(\alpha\).

\[ \mathbf z_0 \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf z_b, \mathbf B), \qquad \theta \sim \mathcal N(\theta_b, \boldsymbol\Sigma_\theta). \]

Dictionary to the figure. The figure writes the generic problem as \(y = H(f(\theta)) + \varepsilon\), where \(\theta\) is "the quantity we want". For ocean state estimation that quantity is the unknown initial state and parameters, the pair \((\mathbf z_0, \theta)\). The figure's \(f\) is the trajectory map \((\mathbf z_0,\theta) \mapsto (\mathcal F^{(1)}_\theta(\mathbf z_0),\dots,\mathcal F^{(K)}_\theta(\mathbf z_0))\), and its \(H\) is the stack of the \(\mathbf H_k\). The figure's θ is therefore our \((\mathbf z_0, \theta)\), and its "inner loop: x, θ" means our \(\mathbf z\) and \(\theta\). The covariates \(\mathbf x\) and controls \(\mathbf u\) are inside \(f\): known inputs, not unknowns.

The objects above define a state-space model, a hidden Markov chain with Gaussian noise. Read top to bottom, it is a recipe for simulating an ocean and the satellite data it would produce:

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta &\sim \mathcal N(\theta_b, \boldsymbol\Sigma_\theta) &&\text{draw the physics}\\ \mathbf z_0 &\sim \mathcal N(\mathbf z_b, \mathbf B) &&\text{draw an initial sea level}\\ \mathbf z_k &= \mathcal F_\theta(\mathbf z_{k-1};\,\mathbf x,\mathbf u_k) + \mathbf w_k, && k = 1,\dots,K \quad\text{run the QG model}\\ \mathbf y_k &= \mathbf H_k \mathbf z_k + \boldsymbol\varepsilon_k, && k = 1,\dots,K \quad\text{observe it} \end{aligned} \]

The recipe is equivalent to a joint density, which is everything step 2 needs:

\[ p(\mathbf z_{0:K}, \theta, \mathbf y_{1:K}) = p(\theta)\,p(\mathbf z_0)\prod_{k=1}^{K} \underbrace{p(\mathbf z_k \mid \mathbf z_{k-1}, \theta)}_{\mathcal N(\mathcal F_\theta(\mathbf z_{k-1}),\,\mathbf Q)}\;\underbrace{p(\mathbf y_k \mid \mathbf z_k)}_{\mathcal N(\mathbf H_k\mathbf z_k,\,\mathbf R_k)} . \]

The arrow from step 1 to steps 3 and 5 in the figure is this recipe run many times. In DA vocabulary that is an observing system simulation experiment (OSSE): a "nature run" \(\mathbf z^{\text{true}}_{0:K}\) plus synthetic maps \(\mathbf y_{1:K}\) made with the real \(\mathbf H_k\) and \(\mathbf R_k\). Each OSSE gives a pair \((\mathbf z_{0:K}, \mathbf y_{1:K})\) whose answer is known exactly.

The exact case: the Kalman filter

If \(\mathcal F_\theta\) were linear, \(\mathcal F_\theta(\mathbf z) = \mathbf F\mathbf z\), every density above would stay Gaussian and the filter would be exact. Write \(\mathbf z^f_k, \mathbf P^f_k\) for the forecast mean and covariance and \(\mathbf z^a_k, \mathbf P^a_k\) for the analysis:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{forecast:}\quad & \mathbf z^f_k = \mathbf F\mathbf z^a_{k-1}, && \mathbf P^f_k = \mathbf F\mathbf P^a_{k-1}\mathbf F^\top + \mathbf Q,\\ \text{gain:}\quad & \mathbf K_k = \mathbf P^f_k\mathbf H_k^\top\big(\mathbf H_k\mathbf P^f_k\mathbf H_k^\top + \mathbf R_k\big)^{-1}, &&\\ \text{analysis:}\quad & \mathbf z^a_k = \mathbf z^f_k + \mathbf K_k\big(\mathbf y_k - \mathbf H_k\mathbf z^f_k\big), && \mathbf P^a_k = (\mathbf I - \mathbf K_k\mathbf H_k)\mathbf P^f_k . \end{aligned} \]

Two facts kill it for the ocean. QG advection \(J(\psi,q)\) is quadratic, so \(\mathcal F_\theta\) is nonlinear. And \(\mathbf P^f_k\) has \(N^2 \approx 2.7\times 10^8\) entries (about 1 GB in float32) and propagating it costs \(N\) tangent-linear runs per day. Every practical method below drops one of these two ingredients: 4D-Var drops the covariance, the EnKF drops the full rank.

3D-Var: one analysis as an optimisation

For a single time, the Kalman analysis mean is also the minimiser of a quadratic cost. Write \(\|\mathbf v\|^2_{\mathbf A} = \mathbf v^\top\mathbf A\mathbf v\):

\[ \mathcal J_{\text{3D}}(\mathbf z) = \tfrac12\|\mathbf z - \mathbf z_b\|^2_{\mathbf B^{-1}} + \tfrac12\|\mathbf y - \mathbf H\mathbf z\|^2_{\mathbf R^{-1}} . \]

This is \(-\log\) of prior times likelihood, so its minimiser is the MAP estimate. With \(\mathbf z_b = \mathbf z^f\) and \(\mathbf B = \mathbf P^f\) it equals \(\mathbf z^a\) exactly. 3D-Var ignores time: it is the L2 → L3 optimal interpolation written as a cost function.

Strong-constraint 4D-Var: the whole window through the model

Assume the model is perfect, \(\mathbf Q = \mathbf 0\). Then the trajectory is fixed by \(\mathbf z_0\) and \(\theta\), and the negative log posterior is a function of those alone:

\[ \mathcal J(\mathbf z_0,\theta) = \underbrace{\tfrac12\|\mathbf z_0 - \mathbf z_b\|^2_{\mathbf B^{-1}}}_{\text{background}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\|\theta - \theta_b\|^2_{\boldsymbol\Sigma_\theta^{-1}}}_{\text{parameters}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big\|\mathbf y_k - \mathbf H_k\mathcal F^{(k)}_\theta(\mathbf z_0)\big\|^2_{\mathbf R_k^{-1}}}_{\text{misfit to 10 maps}} . \]

The minimiser \((\mathbf z_0^\star,\theta^\star)\) is the MAP estimate, and running the model from it gives the analysed trajectory. The minimisation needs \(\nabla_{\mathbf z_0}\mathcal J\). Let \(\mathbf d_k = \mathbf R_k^{-1}\big(\mathbf y_k - \mathbf H_k\mathbf z_k\big)\) be the weighted misfit on day \(k\). Run the adjoint backwards, starting from \(\boldsymbol\lambda_{K+1} = \mathbf 0\):

\[ \boldsymbol\lambda_k = \mathbf H_k^\top\mathbf d_k + \mathbf F_{k+1}^\top\boldsymbol\lambda_{k+1},\quad k = K,\dots,1, \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf z_0}\mathcal J = \mathbf B^{-1}(\mathbf z_0 - \mathbf z_b) - \mathbf F_1^\top\boldsymbol\lambda_1 . \]

The backward recursion is reverse-mode automatic differentiation of the model. In JAX it is jax.grad of \(\mathcal J\); in a Fortran ocean model it is a separately maintained adjoint code. One gradient costs about one forward run plus one adjoint run, two to three forward runs in total. Adding \(\theta\) to the control vector, as above, is parameter estimation by the same machinery.

In practice the optimisation variable is changed to \(\mathbf z_0 = \mathbf z_b + \mathbf B^{1/2}\boldsymbol\xi\), so the background term becomes \(\tfrac12\|\boldsymbol\xi\|^2\) and the problem is well conditioned. Applying \(\mathbf B^{1/2}\) matrix-free (a Kronecker or spectral factor of the Matérn kernel) is a linear-algebra job, which is where gaussx sits.

Op = lx.AbstractLinearOperator


class Window(eqx.Module):
    """One 10-day window: maps y, masks m, errors σ², background z_b, priors."""

    maps: Float[Array, "time height width"]
    masks: Bool[Array, "time height width"]
    obs_var: Float[Array, "time height width"]
    background: Float[Array, "cell"]
    covariates: Float[Array, "variable height width"]
    forcing: Float[Array, "time height width"]
    prior_cov: Op
    param_mean: Float[Array, "param"]
    param_cov: Op
    grid: fvx.CartesianGrid2D


def strong_4dvar_cost(
    control: tuple[Float[Array, "cell"], Float[Array, "param"]],
    window: Window,
) -> Float[Array, ""]:
    whitened, params = control
    height = window.maps.shape[1]
    # user (taxonomy page)
    qg_params = QGParams(deformation_radius=params[0], viscosity=params[1])

    # --- background ---
    # today* (gaussx)
    # B^{1/2}
    prior_sqrt = gx.sqrt(window.prior_cov)            # (N, N) PSD op
    # z₀ − z_b = B^{1/2} ξ
    increment = prior_sqrt.mv(whitened)               # (N,) → (N,)
    # z₀ = z_b + B^{1/2} ξ
    initial_flat = window.background + increment      # (N,) → (N,)
    initial_state = einx.rearrange(                   # (N,) → (H, W)
        "(height width) -> height width", initial_flat, height=height
    )
    # ‖ξ‖²
    whitened_norm = einx.dot("cell, cell ->", whitened, whitened)  # → ()
    # J_b = ½‖ξ‖²
    background_cost = 0.5 * whitened_norm             # → ()

    # --- parameters ---
    # θ − θ_b
    param_departure = params - window.param_mean      # (P,) → (P,)
    # today (gaussx)
    # (θ − θ_b)ᵀ Σ_θ⁻¹ (θ − θ_b)
    param_quad = gx.quadratic_form(window.param_cov, param_departure)  # → ()
    # J_θ = ½‖θ − θ_b‖²_{Σ_θ⁻¹}
    param_cost = 0.5 * param_quad                     # → ()

    # --- observations, one day per scan step ---
    def assimilate_day(state, day):
        observed_map, mask, obs_var, daily_forcing = day
        # user (qg_day, taxonomy page)
        # z_k = 𝓕_θ(z_{k−1}; x, u_k), 48 RK4 steps
        state = qg_day(                               # (H, W) → (H, W)
            state, window.covariates, daily_forcing, qg_params, window.grid
        )
        # y_k − z_k
        misfit = observed_map - state                 # (H, W)
        # r_k = m_k ⊙ (y_k − z_k)
        residual = jnp.where(mask, misfit, 0.0)       # (H, W)
        # r_k²
        squared = residual**2                         # (H, W)
        # r_k² / σ_k²
        weighted = squared / obs_var                  # (H, W)
        # ½ ∑ r_k² / σ_k²
        day_sum = einx.sum("height width ->", weighted)  # → ()
        day_cost = 0.5 * day_sum                      # → ()
        return state, day_cost

    days = (window.maps, window.masks, window.obs_var, window.forcing)
    # J_o = ∑_k ½‖m_k ⊙ (y_k − z_k)‖²_{R_k⁻¹}
    _, day_costs = jax.lax.scan(assimilate_day, initial_state, days)  # → (T,)
    obs_cost = einx.sum("time ->", day_costs)         # (T,) → ()

    # 𝒥 = J_b + J_θ + J_o
    total_cost = background_cost + param_cost + obs_cost  # → ()
    return total_cost


# not pinned (optimistix)
# (ξ*, θ*) = argmin 𝒥(ξ, θ); ∇𝒥 by reverse mode is the adjoint recursion
solution = optx.minimise(                             # → ((N,), (P,))
    strong_4dvar_cost,
    optx.BFGS(rtol=1e-6, atol=1e-6),
    (whitened_init, params_init),
    args=window,
)
whitened_star, params_star = solution.value

Weak-constraint 4D-Var: admitting the model is wrong

With \(\mathbf Q \neq \mathbf 0\) the whole trajectory becomes the control variable, and the cost is exactly the negative log of the smoothing posterior:

\[ \mathcal J(\mathbf z_{0:K}) = \tfrac12\|\mathbf z_0 - \mathbf z_b\|^2_{\mathbf B^{-1}} + \tfrac12\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big\|\mathbf z_k - \mathcal F_\theta(\mathbf z_{k-1})\big\|^2_{\mathbf Q^{-1}} + \tfrac12\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big\|\mathbf y_k - \mathbf H_k\mathbf z_k\big\|^2_{\mathbf R_k^{-1}} . \]

Strong-constraint 4D-Var is the limit \(\mathbf Q \to \mathbf 0\). The control vector grows from \(N\) to \((K+1)N\), but the terms decouple in time, so the days can be evaluated in parallel.

Uncertainty from 4D-Var

4D-Var returns a mode, not a distribution. The Laplace approximation recovers a Gaussian around it, with the Gauss–Newton Hessian. Let \(\mathbf G_k = \partial\mathbf z_k / \partial\mathbf z_0 = \mathbf F_k\cdots\mathbf F_1\):

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf z_0 \mid \mathbf y_{1:K}) \approx \Big(\mathbf B^{-1} + \sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf G_k^\top\mathbf H_k^\top\mathbf R_k^{-1}\mathbf H_k\mathbf G_k\Big)^{-1}. \]

It is never formed. Each Hessian–vector product is one tangent-linear plus one adjoint run, and Lanczos extracts its leading eigenpairs: the directions the ten maps actually constrain.

The ensemble Kalman filter: sampling the covariance

The EnKF keeps the Kalman equations and replaces \(\mathbf P^f_k\) with a sample. Run \(N_e\) members \(\mathbf z^{(i)}_k\), say \(N_e = 32\), each through the full nonlinear model. Stack the scaled anomalies into \(\mathbf X'_k \in \mathbb R^{N\times N_e}\):

\[ \mathbf X'_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_e-1}}\big[\mathbf z^{(1)}_k - \bar{\mathbf z}_k,\;\dots,\;\mathbf z^{(N_e)}_k - \bar{\mathbf z}_k\big], \qquad \mathbf P^f_k \approx \mathbf X'_k\mathbf X'^{\top}_k . \]

The gain becomes \(\mathbf K_k = \mathbf X'_k(\mathbf H_k\mathbf X'_k)^\top\big(\mathbf H_k\mathbf X'_k(\mathbf H_k\mathbf X'_k)^\top + \mathbf R_k\big)^{-1}\), and each member is updated with it. The matrix to invert is \(p_k\times p_k\), or \(N_e\times N_e\) in the ensemble-transform form. The sample covariance has rank at most \(N_e - 1 = 31\) against \(N = 16\,384\), so two fixes are always needed. Localisation multiplies the covariance elementwise by a compact taper \(\boldsymbol\rho\) (for example 150 km), \(\mathbf P^f \approx \boldsymbol\rho\circ\mathbf X'\mathbf X'^\top\), which removes spurious long-range correlations. Inflation scales the anomalies by \(\alpha \gtrsim 1\) to stop the spread collapsing. Parameters are estimated by appending \(\theta\) to each member's state. The ensemble smoother (EnKS) gives the reanalysis.

Look at where the cost of step 2 lives: in \(\mathcal F_\theta\), evaluated hundreds of times per iteration, and in its adjoint. Step 3 replaces that one object. A network \(\widehat{\mathcal F}_\phi\) with weights \(\phi\) learns the one-day map from the step-1 nature runs:

\[ \phi^\star = \arg\min_\phi\; \mathbb E_{\theta,\,\mathbf z_0\sim p}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big\|\mathbf z_k - \widehat{\mathcal F}^{(k)}_\phi(\mathbf z_0;\theta)\big\|^2, \qquad \mathbf z_k = \mathcal F^{(k)}_\theta(\mathbf z_0). \]

With \(K = 1\) this is one-step training; with \(K > 1\) it is a rollout loss (somax's rollout_loss on a scanned cycle). Its reference is step 1: the emulator is scored against the simulator it replaces, not against data.

The figure's "skip if cheap" applies here. One emulator call replaces 48 QG steps, but 48 QG steps at 128² already take milliseconds in JAX, and the QG model is already differentiable. For this running example the emulator buys little. It pays off when \(\mathcal F\) is a large ocean model with no adjoint.

What the formulation demands of the emulator

4D-Var uses the adjoint \(\mathbf F_k^\top\), not only \(\mathcal F\). A network that matches the forward map can still have the wrong Jacobian, and then the gradients in step 4 point the wrong way. The objective above only constrains values. If the emulator is meant for step 4, add a Jacobian term with random directions \(\mathbf v\): \(\big\|\mathbf F_k\mathbf v - \widehat{\mathbf F}_k\mathbf v\big\|^2\), two JVPs per sample. An ensemble method in step 4 needs forward fidelity only.

Same outputs as box 2, but no z_b and no α among the inputs: the prior is learned from the training set, not passed in.

Steps 2 and 4 solve a new optimisation for every window. Step 5 learns the answer as a function of the data. A conditional density \(q_\psi\) with weights \(\psi\) is trained on step-1 OSSE pairs:

\[ \psi^\star = \arg\min_\psi\; \mathbb E_{(\mathbf z_{0:K},\,\mathbf y_{1:K})\sim p}\big[-\log q_\psi(\mathbf z_{0:K}\mid \mathbf y_{1:K})\big], \qquad q_{\psi^\star}(\mathbf z_{0:K}\mid\mathbf y_{1:K}) \approx p(\mathbf z_{0:K}\mid\mathbf y_{1:K}). \]

Minimising this expectation minimises \(\mathbb E_{\mathbf y}\big[\mathrm{KL}\big(p(\cdot\mid\mathbf y)\,\|\,q_\psi(\cdot\mid\mathbf y)\big)\big]\), so a flexible \(q_\psi\) converges to the true posterior. The simplest case is a Gaussian with fixed variance and learned mean \(g_\psi(\mathbf y)\). The loss becomes mean squared error, whose minimiser is the posterior mean:

\[ \min_\psi\; \mathbb E\,\big\|\mathbf z_{0:K} - g_\psi(\mathbf y_{1:K})\big\|^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g_{\psi^\star}(\mathbf y_{1:K}) = \mathbb E\big[\mathbf z_{0:K}\mid\mathbf y_{1:K}\big]. \]

That is the "direct analysis": a network from ten SSH maps to the state, at the cost of one forward pass.

Three consequences of the formulation

Mean versus mode. An MSE-trained network estimates the posterior mean, while 4D-Var returns the posterior mode. They coincide only in the linear-Gaussian case. Compare step 5 with the step-2 ensemble mean, or with 4D-Var's mode only after checking the posterior is close to Gaussian.

The prior is implicit. Steps 2 and 4 state \(\mathbf B\) and \(p(\theta)\) explicitly. Step 5 learns its prior from the distribution of nature runs. If those runs do not look like the real Gulf Stream, the amortized posterior is wrong in a way no single-window check reveals.

The observing system is part of the input. \(\mathbf H_k\) and \(\mathbf R_k\) change every day (clouds do not matter for altimetry, but coverage, land masks and pixel errors do). The network must see the mask and error map as inputs, \(q_\psi(\mathbf z\mid \mathbf y,\mathbf H,\mathbf R)\), and training must sample them, or it will fail on the first unusual day.

The formulation contains its own test. If steps 1 and 2 are right, the innovations, \(\mathbf d_k = \mathbf y_k - \mathbf H_k\mathbf z^f_k\), must be zero-mean with a known covariance, because they are the sum of two independent Gaussian errors:

\[ \mathbb E[\mathbf d_k] = \mathbf 0, \qquad \mathbb E\big[\mathbf d_k\mathbf d_k^\top\big] = \mathbf H_k\mathbf P^f_k\mathbf H_k^\top + \mathbf R_k . \]

Two scalar checks follow. With \(p = \sum_k p_k\) observations, the minimum of a correctly specified cost satisfies \(\mathbb E\big[2\,\mathcal J(\mathbf z^\star)\big]\approx p\), so \(2\mathcal J^\star/p\) should be near 1. These checks are how the hyperparameters \(\alpha\) get tuned. The Desroziers identity checks \(\mathbf R\) on its own: \(\mathbb E\big[(\mathbf y - \mathbf H\mathbf z^a)(\mathbf y - \mathbf H\mathbf z^f)^\top\big]\approx\mathbf R\).
